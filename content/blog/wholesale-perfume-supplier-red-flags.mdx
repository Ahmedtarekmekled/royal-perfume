The fastest way to lose money on a wholesale fragrance order isn't a bad product — it's a supplier who was never going to deliver one. Fake and unreliable "wholesale" sellers are common enough in this category that knowing the warning signs before you pay a deposit matters more than almost any other part of sourcing.

## 1. Prices meaningfully below the rest of the market

If one supplier's quote is dramatically lower than everyone else's for the same fragrance at the same volume, ask why before you ask how fast they can ship. Sometimes there's a real explanation — clearing old stock, a genuine manufacturer discount. More often, it means counterfeit product, misrepresented volume, or a seller who has no intention of shipping at all. A price that looks too good to be true in wholesale fragrance almost always is.

## 2. No real product photos, only catalog or stock images

A legitimate supplier — especially a manufacturer — can send an actual photo of the product on request, not a glossy catalog image pulled from the internet. If every image they provide looks like marketing material rather than something photographed in their own warehouse, that's worth noticing. Ask directly for a current photo of the SKU you're ordering, or a sample if the order size justifies it.

## 3. Pressure to pay the full amount upfront with no confirmation step

A supplier experienced in international wholesale should be able to confirm final pricing and shipping cost with you directly before collecting payment — not lock in a number automatically and expect full payment before you've reviewed it. Pressure to wire the full balance immediately, especially combined with urgency ("this price is only good today"), is a common scam pattern. A real supplier can wait for you to confirm details.

## 4. No verifiable business history

Search for the company. A supplier with years of transaction history, reviews, or a documented presence has something to lose by scamming you. A brand-new account, a generic email address, or a business with no findable footprint outside of the platform you found them on carries meaningfully more risk — not a guarantee of fraud, but a reason to ask more questions before committing capital.

## 5. Vague or evasive answers about manufacturer vs. reseller status

Ask directly: are they a manufacturer, or are they reselling stock from someone else? Neither answer is automatically disqualifying, but a supplier who dodges the question or gives inconsistent answers between conversations is a bigger risk than one who answers clearly either way. If they're a reseller, ask where they source from — a supplier who can't or won't say is worth walking away from.

## 6. No clear return or damage policy

Ask what happens if a shipment arrives short, damaged, or doesn't match the order — before you pay, not after it happens. A supplier without a clear answer, or one who deflects the question entirely, is telling you something about how they'll handle a problem if one comes up. This is a low-cost question to ask early and a high-cost one to skip.

## 7. Communication only through unofficial or unverifiable channels

Legitimate wholesale suppliers are generally reachable through consistent, verifiable channels — a business WhatsApp or Telegram tied to a real storefront, an email domain matching their website, or a documented company account. If every interaction happens through a personal account with no other trace of the business, treat that as a reason for more caution, not less.

## The due-diligence questions worth asking any supplier before you pay a deposit

- Are you a manufacturer or a reseller? Where does your stock come from if you're a reseller?
- Can I see an actual product photo or sample, not a catalog image?
- Is the shipping cost and final price confirmed with me before I pay, or charged automatically?
- What happens if a shipment arrives short or damaged?
- What's your real MOQ, and how does pricing change above or below it?

A supplier who answers all five clearly and consistently is a fundamentally different risk profile than one who dodges even one of them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a low wholesale price always a scam?**
Not always, but it's always worth a direct question. Legitimate discounts happen — clearing stock, a manufacturer promotion — but a price far below the rest of the market with no clear explanation is one of the most common scam signals in this category.

**Should I ever pay the full amount before receiving a sample?**
For a first order with a new supplier, it's reasonable to ask for a sample or real product photos first, and to expect a confirmation step on final pricing and shipping before you pay. A supplier unwilling to offer either is a bigger risk than a slightly higher price from one who will.

**How can I verify a supplier is really a manufacturer?**
Ask specific questions about their facility, how long they've been producing, and how they control batch consistency. A real manufacturer can answer these in detail; a reseller pretending to be one usually can't.

**What should I do if a shipment arrives short or damaged?**
Contact the supplier immediately with photos and documentation. A legitimate supplier will have a clear return or replacement process already in place — if they don't, that's a red flag worth remembering for your next order.
