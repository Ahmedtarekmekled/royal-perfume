Most people who try to start a fragrance reselling business make the same mistake: they place an order before they've decided what business they're actually running. Fragrance resale isn't one business — it's several different ones wearing the same label, and which one you pick determines everything downstream, from how much capital you need to who your actual customer is.

## Decide which business you're building before you order anything

**Retail resale** means selling individual bottles to end consumers — online marketplaces, a storefront, social selling. Margins per unit are highest here, but so is the work: photography, customer service, returns, and competing directly with brands that have far more marketing budget than you.

**Wholesale-to-retail** means selling in bulk to shops, salons, or other small retailers who resell to consumers themselves. Lower margin per unit, but volume is the point — a single order can be 50-500 units instead of one at a time, and you're never dealing with an individual customer's return request.

**Private label / white label** means putting your own brand on someone else's formulation, selling it as your own line. This is the highest-commitment path — you're building a brand, not just moving stock — and it only makes sense once you already understand what sells in your market.

Pick one to start. Trying to run all three from a single first order is how new resellers end up with capital tied up in the wrong SKUs.

## Sizing your first order without guessing

The instinct is to order broad — a little of everything — to "see what sells." This is usually the wrong move. A narrow first order in a category you actually understand (a specific audience, a specific price tier, a specific scent family) gives you a real signal about demand. A broad order gives you a warehouse of things you can't explain why you bought.

A more reliable approach:

1. **Pick 3-5 SKUs you'd bet on**, not 30 you're unsure about.
2. **Ask your supplier about their MOQ tiers before you decide quantity** — most wholesale suppliers price in bands (a 50-unit order and a 500-unit order are different conversations), so your budget should follow the tier that makes sense for your test, not the other way around.
3. **Leave room to reorder fast** on whatever sells, rather than sinking the whole budget into one large first order.

## Pricing without guessing

New resellers often price by copying whatever number they see online for a similar product, without knowing what's actually built into that number. Before you set a retail price, you need to know your own real landed cost — unit cost, plus shipping to you, plus any duties, plus your own packaging or handling. Only after that do you have a number worth marking up.

From there, retail and wholesale pricing behave differently:

- **If you're selling retail**, your margin needs to cover marketing, returns, and your own time — not just the difference between what you paid and what you charge.
- **If you're selling wholesale-to-retail**, your buyer needs enough margin left over to mark it up again themselves. Price too close to your own cost and no retailer can make the math work on their end.

## Legal and compliance — check locally, don't assume

Business registration requirements, import duties, and cosmetics/fragrance labeling rules vary significantly by country and even by region within a country. This isn't something a generic guide can responsibly answer for you — check your local requirements (business registration, import licensing, product labeling) before you commit to your first order, not after stock is already in transit.

## Due diligence questions worth asking any supplier

- Are you a manufacturer or a reseller yourself? (Neither is automatically wrong, but you should know which one you're paying.)
- What's the real MOQ, and does pricing change meaningfully above or below it?
- Can I see an actual product photo or sample, not just a catalog image?
- Is the shipping cost and final price confirmed with me before I pay, or charged automatically?
- What happens if a shipment arrives short or damaged?

A supplier who can't answer these clearly is a bigger risk than a slightly higher price from one who can.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a business license to start reselling fragrances?**
Requirements vary by country and sometimes by region — check your local business registration and import rules before ordering. This isn't something to assume based on what you've read elsewhere.

**How much money do I need to start?**
That depends entirely on which business model you pick and your supplier's MOQ tiers. A narrow first order in a specific category will always need less capital than a broad "one of everything" order — and gives you a clearer signal about what to reorder.

**Is it better to start with designer-inspired or niche fragrances?**
Neither is universally better — it depends on your target customer's price sensitivity and what's already saturated in your specific market. Test narrow, watch what actually sells, then expand into the category that performed.
